1 Introduction ¶

tempo is a library for date and time manipulation, not unlike local-time. It attempts to bring a more ergonomic and modern experience with conditions, restarts, and predictable naming.

  • Scope
  • Installing and loading
  • Five-minute tour

Next: Installing and loading, Up: Introduction   [Contents][Index]

1.1 Scope ¶

The core distinction in tempo is between calendar values and elapsed time:

Calendar values (date, wall-time, datetime)

Positions on a calendar or clock. These values are naive: they carry no timezone information.

Elapsed time (duration)

A displacement between two points in time. tempo models elapsed time with two independent slots: integer calendar months and exact rational seconds.

The types in tempo are structure values with read-only slots; the public API never mutates them. They compare with equalp and work as :test 'equalp hash-table keys.

tempo deliberately never guesses a timezone, a month length, or a rounding policy. When an operation requires information the value cannot supply, it signals a condition or offers a restart. Domain errors are reported through specific subtypes of tempo-error, while type mismatches signal direct type-error.

Zoned instants (timestamp) and ranges (interval) are planned for later phases (see Limits and roadmap).

Next: Five-minute tour, Previous: Scope, Up: Introduction   [Contents][Index]

1.2 Installing and loading ¶

tempo is not yet on quicklisp, you can install it locally by cloning the repo to your local-projects directory, which is ~/quicklisp/local-projects/ by default. 

git clone https://github.com/raynei86/tempo.git ~/quicklisp/local-projects

Or alternatively via roswell: 

ros install raynei86/tempo

Then it can be loaded through ASDF: 

(asdf:load-system "tempo")

⇒ #<DATE 2026-10-30>

(tempo:wall-time 9 30)
⇒ #<WALL-TIME 09:30:00>

(tempo:datetime 2026 10 30 9 30)
⇒ #<DATETIME 2026-10-30T09:30:00>

(tempo:seconds 90)
⇒ #<DURATION PT1M30S>

(tempo:datetime-difference
 (tempo:datetime 2026 10 31 9 30)

⇒ #<DURATION P1D>

The printed forms are for humans. They assume that tempo is accessible in the current package (for example, because your package uses it); if it is not, your Lisp may qualify the structure type with its home package. For storage and interchange, use the ISO formatters described in Formatting.

2 Choosing a type ¶

The first decision is whether you are storing a calendar position or a displacement. The second is whether a timezone is part of the value. If two types seem to fit, prefer the smaller one: a birthday is a date, not a midnight datetime; opening hours are a wall-time, not a datetime on an arbitrary day. Using the smaller type prevents accidental timezone and daylight-saving assumptions.

3.1 Exactness ¶

tempo stores time as exact rationals, not as floating-point numbers. The unit constructors accept any real number at the boundary and calls rationalize on it: 

(tempo:duration-seconds (tempo:seconds 0.1))
⇒ 1/10
(tempo:duration-seconds (tempo:seconds (rational 0.1)))
⇒ 13421773/134217728

rationalize approximates a float into a rational representation within its rounding precision; thus 0.1 becomes 1/10. If you need the exact mathematical value of a float, use rational instead.

Next: Storage and display, Previous: Exactness, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.2 Months are not seconds ¶

A duration has two independent slots: integer calendar months and exact seconds. The slots are not interchangeable, because a month has no fixed length. Asking for seconds when the value has months signals inexact-duration-error: 

(tempo:in-seconds (tempo:months 1))
⇒ error: INEXACT-DURATION-ERROR

The same rule applies in the other direction: in-months and in-years require the seconds slot to be zero. A year is twelve months, so (tempo:years 1) and (tempo:months 12) are the same duration: 

(tempo:duration= (tempo:years 1) (tempo:months 12))
⇒ T

But a month is not thirty days. duration= compares the slots literally, while ordering needs a common unit: 

(tempo:duration= (tempo:months 1) (tempo:days 30))
⇒ NIL

(tempo:duration< (tempo:months 1) (tempo:days 30)) signals inexact-duration-error, because months and seconds cannot be ordered without a reference date. Use duration= when you mean “the same two slots”, and use calendar arithmetic when you mean “the same amount of time from this date”.

Next: Signs, Previous: Months are not seconds, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.3 Storage and display ¶

Storage keeps the two slots separate. Time units are converted to seconds within the seconds slot, while calendar months stay in the months slot; display decomposes both for readability. A duration of thirteen months prints as one year and one month, but the stored months slot is still thirteen: 

(tempo:duration :months 13)
⇒ #<DURATION P1Y1M>

(tempo:duration-months (tempo:duration :months 13))
⇒ 13

Likewise, ninety minutes are stored as 5400 seconds and printed with hours and minutes: 

(tempo:duration :minutes 90)
⇒ #<DURATION PT1H30M>

(tempo:duration-seconds (tempo:duration :minutes 90))
⇒ 5400

destructure-duration truncates toward zero and returns the components in ISO order: 

(tempo:destructure-duration (tempo:duration :months 13 :days 1))
⇒ 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0

Do not rely on the printed form to recover the slots. Read duration-months and duration-seconds, or use destructure-duration when you want a human decomposition.

Next: Calendar conventions, Previous: Storage and display, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.4 Signs ¶

A negative duration is written with a leading minus sign: 

(tempo:format-duration-iso (tempo:duration :seconds -10))
⇒ "-PT10S"

A duration may have a positive months slot and a negative seconds slot, or the reverse. Such a value has no overall sign, so duration-plusp, duration-minusp, and duration-abs signal inexact-duration-error. Avoid constructing mixed-sign durations; if you need a signed displacement, keep the same sign on both slots.

Next: Naive time, Previous: Signs, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.5 Calendar conventions ¶

tempo uses the proleptic Gregorian calendar. Year 0 exists and is a leap year, and the constructors accept any integer year, including negative years: 

(tempo:valid-date-p 0 2 29)
⇒ T

(tempo:leap-year-p 0)
⇒ T

(tempo:date -221 1 1)
⇒ #<TEMPO.DATE:DATE -221-01-01>

ISO 8601 formatting is only defined for years 0000 through 9999; see Formatting types.

Weekdays are numbered 1 through 7, with Monday as 1 and Sunday as 7. Weeks run Monday through Sunday: 

(tempo:day-of-week (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
⇒ 5

(tempo:start-of-week (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
⇒ #<DATE 2026-10-26>

(tempo:end-of-week (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
⇒ #<DATE 2026-11-01>

next-weekday and previous-weekday are strict: if the date already falls on the requested weekday, they move to the next or previous week. This is usually what calendar code wants, and it makes the “nth weekday” recipe simple.

ISO week numbers are not the same as calendar year and day-of-year. iso-calendar returns three values, and the ISO year can differ from the calendar year at the boundary: 

(multiple-value-list (tempo:iso-calendar (tempo:date 2021 1 1)))
⇒ (2020 53 5)

January 1, 2021 is a Friday in ISO week 53 of 2020.

rata-die numbers days so that 0001-01-01 is day 1 and 0000-12-31 is day 0. Day numbers are the exact, timezone-free way to add days and measure spans: 

(tempo:rata-die (tempo:date 0 12 31))
⇒ 0

Next: Equality, Previous: Calendar conventions, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.6 Naive time ¶

A datetime has no timezone information. Two datetimes with the same fields are equalp even if one is interpreted in Tokyo and the other in New York, and comparing them says nothing about which instant is earlier.

today, yesterday, and tomorrow read the process’s local timezone through get-decoded-time. They are convenience functions, not a clock protocol; a test that depends on them is timezone-dependent and can fail around midnight. 

(tempo:today)
⇒ #<DATE ...>

If you need an exact instant, wait for timestamp, or convert explicitly and record the zone alongside the value. Do not order datetimes from different zones.

Next: Formatting types, Previous: Naive time, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.7 Equality ¶

The values in tempo are structures with read-only slots. equalp compares their contents; equal compares identity, so it is false for two separately constructed values: 

(equalp (tempo:date 2026 10 30) (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
⇒ T

(equal (tempo:date 2026 10 30) (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
⇒ NIL

Use equalp for hash tables: 

(let ((table (make-hash-table :test 'equalp)))
  (setf (gethash (tempo:date 2026 10 30) table) :friday)
  (gethash (tempo:date 2026 10 30) table))
⇒ :FRIDAY

Previous: Equality, Up: Concepts   [Contents][Index]

3.8 Formatting types ¶

Every type has an ISO 8601 formatter: format-date-iso, format-wall-time-iso, format-datetime-iso, and format-duration-iso. Each also has a stream variant named write-TYPE-to-iso, and a cl:format directive: 

(format nil "at ~/tempo:format-datetime/"
        (tempo:datetime 2026 10 30 9 30))
⇒ "at 2026-10-30T09:30:00"

The printed forms are:

#<DURATION PT1H30M>
#<DATE 2026-10-30>
#<WALL-TIME 09:30:00.5>
#<DATETIME 2026-10-30T09:30:00.5>

print-object for the corresponding values.

ISO formatting is not lossless serializer for every possible value. Two caveats exist:

  • Fractional seconds are printed through a double float. A non-terminating rational such as 1/3 becomes "PT0.3333333333333333S". The value is still exact; only the string is approximate.
  • Date formatting is defined for years 0000 through 9999. The constructors accept any integer year, but years outside that range do not have a conforming ISO string.

For round-tripping your own data, store the ISO string and parse it at the boundary. tempo does not provide a parser yet (see Limits and roadmap).

4.1 Validating input ¶

Use the total predicates at the boundary. They return nil for nonsense instead of signalling: 

(defun safe-date (year month day)
  (when (tempo:valid-date-p year month day)
    (tempo:date year month day)))

(safe-date 2026 2 30)
⇒ NIL

(safe-date 2026 2 28)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-02-28>

When you need to explain why the input was rejected, handle the condition and read its slots: 

(handler-case (tempo:date 2026 2 30)
  (tempo:invalid-date-error (c)
    (list :invalid
          (tempo:invalid-date-month c)
          (tempo:invalid-date-day c)
          (tempo:invalid-date-reason c))))
⇒ (:INVALID 2 30 "February 2026 has 28 days, not 30 days")

The same pattern works for valid-wall-time-p and valid-datetime-p. Remember that a non-integer field signals a plain type-error, not a tempo-error (see Errors and restarts).

Once data is inside your program, stop validating and let the constructors enforce the invariant. Do not pass unchecked user input to date or wall-time and then interpret the resulting condition as a validation result.

Next: Month-end dates, Previous: Validating input, Up: Common tasks   [Contents][Index]

4.2 Ages and birthdays ¶

An age is a calendar calculation, not a division by 365. Compare the month and day in the target year, and decide what February 29 means. The min below clamps February 29 to February 28 in non-leap years: 

(defun birthday-in-year (birthday year)
  (let ((month (tempo:date-month birthday)))
    (tempo:date year
                month
                (min (tempo:date-day birthday)
                     (tempo:days-in-month year month)))))

(defun age-on (birthday on)
  (let ((this-year (birthday-in-year birthday (tempo:date-year on))))
    (if (tempo:date<= this-year on)
        (- (tempo:date-year on) (tempo:date-year birthday))
        (1- (- (tempo:date-year on) (tempo:date-year birthday))))))

(defun next-birthday (birthday today)
  (let ((this-year (birthday-in-year birthday (tempo:date-year today))))
    (if (tempo:date< this-year today)
        (birthday-in-year birthday (1+ (tempo:date-year today)))
        this-year)))

(age-on (tempo:date 2000 2 29) (tempo:date 2026 2 28))
⇒ 26

(next-birthday (tempo:date 2000 2 29) (tempo:date 2026 3 1))
⇒ #<DATE 2027-02-28>

This next-birthday includes today when today is the birthday. If you need strictly later, compare with tempo:date< and roll to the next year when equal.

If your policy is “March 1 in non-leap years” instead, compare the month and day explicitly rather than clamping. The important point is that the policy is in your code, where it can be reviewed.

4.3 Month-end dates ¶

To clamp a day to the end of a month, use replace-date and let the restart rewrite the overflow: 

(defun billing-date (year month day)
  (handler-bind ((tempo:invalid-date-error
                   (lambda (c)
                     (declare (ignore c))
                     (invoke-restart 'tempo:clamp-to-end-of-month))))
    (tempo:replace-date (tempo:date year month 1) :day day)))

(billing-date 2026 2 31)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-02-28>

The restart is offered by replace-date and replace-datetime; see Month-end overflow for the semantics.

When you only need the last day, end-of-month is simpler: 

(tempo:end-of-month (tempo:date 2024 2 10))
⇒ #<DATE 2024-02-29>

See Month-end overflow for the condition and restart details.

4.4 Nth and last weekday ¶

next-weekday is strict, so start one day before the month and step forward. Starting one day after the month makes the backwards version just as short: 

(defun nth-weekday (year month weekday n)
  (loop with d = (tempo:date-from-rata-die
                  (1- (tempo:rata-die (tempo:date year month 1))))
        repeat n
        do (setf d (tempo:next-weekday d weekday))
        finally (return d)))

(defun last-weekday (year month weekday)
  (let ((end (tempo:end-of-month (tempo:date year month 1))))
    (tempo:previous-weekday
     (tempo:date-from-rata-die (1+ (tempo:rata-die end)))
     weekday)))

(nth-weekday 2026 11 :thursday 3)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-11-19>

(last-weekday 2026 11 :friday)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-11-27>

n must be at least 1. A large n can spill into the next month; if that matters, check that the result still has the requested month before using it.

4.5 Adding days and spans ¶

Day numbers make day arithmetic exact and timezone-free: 

(defun date+days (date n)
  (tempo:date-from-rata-die (+ (tempo:rata-die date) n)))

(defun days-between (a b)
  (- (tempo:rata-die a) (tempo:rata-die b)))

(date+days (tempo:date 2026 12 31) 1)
⇒ #<DATE 2027-01-01>

(days-between (tempo:date 2026 3 1) (tempo:date 2026 2 28))
⇒ 1

For second-level arithmetic on a datetime, use datetime+ and datetime-; for the exact span between two datetimes, use datetime-difference. Adding calendar months to a date is a different operation and is not implemented yet (see Limits and roadmap).

Next: Elapsed time, Previous: Adding days and spans, Up: Common tasks   [Contents][Index]

4.6 Opening hours ¶

Wall times compare by seconds since midnight. A half-open interval [opening, closing) handles the usual case, and the comparison wraps when closing is earlier than opening: 

(defun open-p (opening closing now)
  (if (tempo:wall-time<= opening closing)
      (and (tempo:wall-time<= opening now)
           (tempo:wall-time< now closing))
      (or (tempo:wall-time<= opening now)
          (tempo:wall-time< now closing))))

(open-p (tempo:wall-time 9 0) (tempo:wall-time 17 0) (tempo:wall-time 12 0))
⇒ T

(open-p (tempo:wall-time 22 0) (tempo:wall-time 2 0) (tempo:wall-time 1 30))
⇒ T

(open-p (tempo:wall-time 22 0) (tempo:wall-time 2 0) (tempo:wall-time 2 0))
⇒ NIL

This is wall-clock logic. It does not know about daylight-saving transitions, holidays, or the date. If those matter, you need a timezone-aware layer, which tempo does not have yet.

Next: Universal time and Unix epoch, Previous: Opening hours, Up: Common tasks   [Contents][Index]

4.7 Elapsed time ¶

For elapsed time inside one process, use the implementation’s internal clock and convert the difference to an exact duration: 

(let ((start (get-internal-real-time)))
  ;; ... work ...
  (tempo:seconds
   (/ (- (get-internal-real-time) start)
      internal-time-units-per-second)))

The division is exact if the units divide evenly; otherwise the result is an exact rational. get-internal-real-time measures wall-clock time and is suitable for timeouts and benchmarks. get-internal-run-time measures processor time instead.

This is not a source of calendar timestamps. There is no clock protocol yet, so tests that need a fixed “now” must pass the date in.

Next: Sorting and hash keys, Previous: Elapsed time, Up: Common tasks   [Contents][Index]

4.8 Universal time and Unix epoch ¶

Universal time is a non-negative integer count of seconds since 1900-01-01 GMT. The timezone argument to decode-universal-time and encode-universal-time is in hours west of GMT; pass 0 for UTC. These helpers project a universal time to a naive datetime and back: 

(defun datetime-from-universal-time (ut &optional (zone 0))
  (multiple-value-bind (second minute hour day month year)
      (decode-universal-time ut zone)
    (tempo:datetime year month day hour minute second)))

(defun universal-time-from-datetime (dt &optional (zone 0))
  (multiple-value-bind (year month day hour minute second)
      (tempo:destructure-datetime dt)
    (encode-universal-time second minute hour day month year zone)))

(datetime-from-universal-time 0)
⇒ #<DATETIME 1900-01-01T00:00:00>

(universal-time-from-datetime (tempo:datetime 1970 1 1))
⇒ 2208988800

Unix time counts seconds from 1970-01-01 UTC. Computing it from day numbers avoids universal time’s 1900 epoch and works for earlier dates: 

(defun unix-time (dt)
  (+ (* 86400 (- (tempo:rata-die (tempo:datetime-date dt))
                 (tempo:rata-die (tempo:date 1970 1 1))))
     (* 3600 (tempo:datetime-hour dt))
     (* 60 (tempo:datetime-minute dt))
     (tempo:datetime-second dt)))

(defun datetime-from-unix-time (seconds)
  (multiple-value-bind (days rem) (floor seconds 86400)
    (multiple-value-bind (hour rem) (truncate rem 3600)
      (multiple-value-bind (minute second) (truncate rem 60)
        (tempo:datetime-from-parts
         (tempo:date-from-rata-die
          (+ (tempo:rata-die (tempo:date 1970 1 1)) days))
         (tempo:make-wall-time
          :hour hour :minute minute :second second))))))

(unix-time (tempo:datetime 1970 1 1))
⇒ 0

(datetime-from-unix-time 0)
⇒ #<DATETIME 1970-01-01T00:00:00>

Both helpers treat the datetime as UTC. If the value really represents a local time, the conversion is a projection and the zone must be recorded separately. Neither helper accounts for leap seconds; POSIX time and the tempo wall clock both ignore them.

Because universal time is non-negative, dates before 1900 cannot be represented at all. For earlier dates, use the Unix helper or work directly with rata-die.

4.9 Sorting and hash keys ¶

The comparison functions are ordinary two-argument predicates, so they work with sort and remove-duplicates: 

(sort (list (tempo:date 2026 1 2) (tempo:date 2025 12 31))
      #'tempo:date<)
⇒ (#<DATE 2025-12-31> #<DATE 2026-01-02>)

sort is destructive; pass a copy if the original list matters. For deduplication, use the type’s equality predicate: 

(remove-duplicates
 (list (tempo:date 2026 10 30) (tempo:date 2026 10 30))
 :test #'tempo:date=)
⇒ (#<DATE 2026-10-30>)

For hash tables, use equalp: 

(let ((table (make-hash-table :test 'equalp)))
  (setf (gethash (tempo:date 2026 10 30) table) :friday)
  (gethash (tempo:date 2026 10 30) table))
⇒ :FRIDAY

Do not use eq or equal for value lookup unless you deliberately want identity.

4.10 ISO week reporting ¶

iso-calendar returns the ISO year, week, and weekday. The ISO year can differ from the calendar year at the boundary: 

(multiple-value-bind (year week day)
    (tempo:iso-calendar (tempo:date 2021 1 1))
  (format nil "~4,'0d-W~2,'0d-~d" year week day))
⇒ "2020-W53-5"

The weekday value is 1 through 7 with Monday as 1, matching day-of-week. For display, keep the ISO year, not the calendar year; otherwise the week number and the year can disagree.

5 Errors and restarts ¶

tempo separates two tiers of mistakes. A value that is the wrong type signals a plain type-error. A value of the right type that does not name a real date or time signals a subtype of tempo-error, which carries the offending fields and a human-readable reason.

  • What signals what
  • Handling errors
  • Month-end overflow

Next: Handling errors, Up: Errors and restarts   [Contents][Index]

5.1 What signals what ¶

date, make-date

invalid-date-error for an impossible date, type-error for a non-integer.

wall-time, make-wall-time

invalid-time-error for an impossible time, type-error for a non-integer.

datetime, make-datetime

It’s composed of a date and wall-time, so those same errors can bubble up.

replace-date, replace-datetime

invalid-date-error; the clamp-to-end-of-month restart is offered when the day overflows.

in-seconds, in-days, and friends

inexact-duration-error; keep the months and seconds separate.

duration-floor, duration-ceiling, duration-round

inexact-duration-error when the duration has months; snap the seconds slot first.

duration-plusp, duration-minusp, duration-abs

inexact-duration-error for mixed signs; avoid mixed-sign durations.

duration<, duration<=, duration>, duration>=

inexact-duration-error for incomparable units; compare in one unit, or use duration=.

duration/, duration-divide

inexact-duration-error or division-by-zero; check the divisor and keep months whole.

duration with a non-integer :months

type-error; use an integer, or use :years.

Next: Month-end overflow, Previous: What signals what, Up: Errors and restarts   [Contents][Index]

5.2 Handling errors ¶

handler-case unwinds the stack and returns a value, so it can be used for reporting or replacing a failed operation: 

(handler-case (tempo:date 2026 2 30)
  (tempo:invalid-date-error (c)
    (list (tempo:invalid-date-month c)
          (tempo:invalid-date-day c)
          (tempo:invalid-date-reason c))))
⇒ (2 30 "February 2026 has 28 days, not 30 days")

handler-bind runs the handler in the dynamic extent where the condition was signalled, so it can invoke a restart and let the original operation finish with a different policy: 

(handler-bind ((tempo:invalid-date-error
                 (lambda (c)
                   (declare (ignore c))
                   (invoke-restart 'tempo:clamp-to-end-of-month))))
  (tempo:replace-date (tempo:date 2024 1 31) :month 2))
⇒ #<DATE 2024-02-29>

Previous: Handling errors, Up: Errors and restarts   [Contents][Index]

5.3 Month-end overflow ¶

replace-date and replace-datetime validate the resulting date. When the requested day overflows the target month, they signal invalid-date-error and offer the clamp-to-end-of-month restart; the example under Handling errors shows the invocation.

6 Limits and roadmap ¶

  • Current limits
  • Roadmap

Next: Roadmap, Up: Limits and roadmap   [Contents][Index]

6.1 Current limits ¶

See Capability map for the quick answer on individual operations. The deferred work falls into two groups: calendar arithmetic (Phase 3) and zoned time (Phase 4). Everything else the capability map marks “No” is deliberate scope for the current release, not a bug. In particular, there is no ISO parser, no default timezone or test clock, no non-Gregorian calendar, and no leap-second handling; the seconds field is always less than 60.

The known rough edges are documented where they arise: Formatting types for ISO output, Signs for mixed-sign durations, and Naive time for today.

Previous: Current limits, Up: Limits and roadmap   [Contents][Index]

6.2 Roadmap ¶

This is a general roadmap of the project:

Phase 1 (complete)

Package layout, conditions, and exact durations.

Phase 2 (complete)

Naive calendar values: date, wall-time, and datetime, with comparisons, seconds arithmetic, and formatting.

Phase 3

Month-bearing arithmetic, date+ and date-, calendar differences, and the month-end restarts.

Phase 4

timestamp, timezone, interval, a clock protocol, and daylight-saving resolution.

Until then, the workarounds in Common tasks cover day arithmetic, validation, weekdays, and interoperability. If a missing feature is blocking you, that is useful information for the roadmap.

7 Duration ¶

Data Type: duration ¶

An exact span of time with two slots: an integer number of calendar months (a year is 12 months) and a rational number of seconds. The time-based unit constructors accept any real number and rationalize it, so there is no floating point drift; the calendar constructors are stricter (see Unit constructors). 

(tempo:duration :hours 3/2)
⇒ #<DURATION PT1H30M>
(tempo:milliseconds 1)
⇒ #<DURATION PT0.001S>

The default print representation uses ISO 8601. A duration with a months component cannot be converted to a fixed number of seconds and signals inexact-duration-error where exactness is required.

8 Date ¶

Data Type: date ¶

A proleptic Gregorian calendar date. Constructors validate their fields: non-integers signal type-error, while impossible calendar triples such as February 30 signal invalid-date-error with a human-readable reason. Any integer year is accepted, including 0 and negative years. 

(tempo:date 2026 10 30)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-10-30>
(tempo:next-weekday (tempo:date 2026 10 30) :friday)
⇒ #<DATE 2026-11-06>
Data Type: weekday ¶

A weekday keyword from :monday through :sunday, or an abbreviation such as :mon, :tue, :wed, :thu, :fri, :sat, or :sun.

Data Type: weekday-designator ¶

Anything accepted where a weekday is expected: a weekday keyword or an integer from 1 (Monday) through 7 (Sunday).

10 Datetime ¶

Data Type: datetime ¶

A naive local date and time, composing a date and a wall-time. It knows nothing about time zones; comparisons, exact differences, and seconds-level arithmetic require no timezone.
